Big-picture primer

How an app like ChatGPT or Claude works

The full journey, in plain language: everything that happens long before you type to create the model, and everything that happens in real time the moment you hit send. Enough to see the whole picture — the deep math can come later.

00

Two very different clocks

The single most useful idea: an AI app runs on two separate timelines. One happens once, over months, to build the model. The other happens every single time you send a message, in about a second.

The whole lifecycle

Build time — done once, ahead of time, costs millions
1. Pre-trainingRead a huge slice of the internet; learn to predict the next word. Produces a raw "base model".
↓
2. Post-training / alignmentTeach it to follow instructions and be helpful, honest, and safe (SFT + RLHF).
↓
3. Evaluate & freezeTest it, then lock the weights. The finished model is now a fixed file.
↓
4. Deploy to serversLoad those frozen weights onto GPU servers behind an API.
Run time — happens live, on every message you send
5. Your query arrivesYour text is bundled with hidden instructions and the chat history.
↓
6. Real-time inferenceThe GPU server runs the frozen model and generates a reply one token at a time.
↓
7. Stream back & repeatWords stream to your screen. Your next message runs the whole thing again.
The model does not learn from your chat in real time. Training already happened. When you talk to it, the weights are frozen — it's just running a fixed function very fast.

Kick off the discussion

  • Why split it this way? What's fundamentally different about building the model vs. answering one message?
  • If the model is frozen at run time, how does it seem to "remember" what you said earlier in the chat?
01Build time

Pre-training: learning language from the internet

It starts with a model that knows nothing — billions of random numbers (parameters). You show it an enormous amount of text and give it one relentless game: predict the next word.

The next-word game

Input: "The capital of France is ___"
↓ model guesses
Guess: "London"  →  wrong. Nudge the numbers a little.
↓ repeat trillions of times
Eventually: "Paris"  →  right, most of the time.

Do this across trillions of words and, to get good at predicting text, the model is forced to absorb grammar, facts, styles, and a surprising amount of reasoning.

The output of this stage is a base model: great at continuing text, but not yet good at being a helpful assistant. It'll happily complete your sentence instead of answering your question.

Talking point

  • Nobody hand-labels "correct answers" here — the text itself is the answer key. Why is that such a big deal for scaling?
  • This is the step our training decision simulator walks through (data, scale, parallelism).
02Build time

Post-training: turning a text predictor into an assistant

A base model predicts plausible text, but that isn't the same as being useful. Post-training reshapes its behavior in two moves.

Two steps to a helpful assistant

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)Humans write lots of example conversations showing good answers. The model imitates them, learning the "assistant" style.
↓
Learning from preferences (RLHF)Show humans two answers; they pick the better one. Train the model to produce more of what people prefer — helpful, honest, safe.
This is where the model learns to answer instead of just continue, to refuse harmful requests, and to adopt the tone you recognize as "ChatGPT" or "Claude". Same underlying network — different behavior.

Talking point

  • Pre-training teaches knowledge; alignment shapes behavior. Why can't you get the second one just by adding more raw internet text?
03Build time

Freeze, package, and deploy

Once the model passes its evals, its parameters are frozen into a fixed set of files (the "weights"). Nothing about the model changes after this until the next version is trained.

From trained model to a live service

Frozen weightsHundreds of gigabytes of numbers — the finished model.
↓ loaded onto
GPU serversFast chips hold the weights in memory, ready to run the model on demand.
↓ wrapped in
An API + the appThe website/phone app sends your message to the server and shows the reply.
Everything above happens before you ever open the app. From here on, we're on the second clock: what happens when you send a message.
04Run time

You hit send: building the real prompt

Here's a surprise: the model never sees just your message. Before anything runs, the app quietly assembles a bigger prompt — hidden instructions, the earlier conversation, and then your text.

Try it — type a question

Here's what actually gets sent to the model:

System prompt (hidden)You are a helpful assistant. Be clear and concise. Today is Monday.
Conversation so farUser: Hi!  ·  Assistant: Hello! How can I help?
Your new messageHow do I make cold brew coffee?
That hidden system prompt plus the chat history is why the model feels like it "remembers" you: the whole conversation is re-sent every turn. This bundle has to fit inside the model's context window (its maximum readable length).

Talking point

  • If the history is re-sent every message, what happens in a very long chat? (Hint: it can overflow the context window.)
  • Who writes the system prompt, and how much power does it have over the model's behavior?
05Run time

Real-time inference: writing one token at a time

Now the GPU server runs the frozen model on that prompt. This is inference, and it happens in two phases.

Watch it generate

1 · Prefill (read the prompt) 2 · Decode (write the reply)
Press generate to watch the model read your prompt, then write its answer token by token.

Picking the next token — the model scores every option; the app samples one:

Prefill: the model reads your whole prompt in one pass and builds a memory of it (the KV cache). Decode: it then produces the answer one token at a time, each time feeding its own last word back in to pick the next. That loop is why longer answers take longer, and why replies stream in.

Talking point

  • Generation is literally "predict the next token" on repeat — the same game from pre-training. Where does the feeling of reasoning come from?
  • The model samples from its top guesses instead of always taking #1. Why would a little randomness make answers feel more natural?
06Run time

Beyond a single reply: tools, memory, and safety

Modern apps wrap the raw model in extra machinery so it can do more than autocomplete.

Common add-ons around the model

Retrieval (RAG)Look up relevant documents and paste them into the prompt, so the model can answer from sources it wasn't trained on.
Tools / function callingLet the model call a calculator, search the web, or run code — then read the result and keep going.
Safety & moderationExtra checks on the input and the output to catch harmful content before it reaches you.
Streaming & the loopTokens stream to your screen; your next message restarts the whole run-time path from step 4.
Put it together: months of training create a frozen brain; a fast serving stack runs it in real time; and a ring of tools and guardrails turns that brain into a product you can actually chat with.
